From August to December 2019, I got to spend half an academic year in another country with my friends. It grew my understanding of other cultures and of how to thrive among very different people.

Mombasa was a lovely city. It reminded me of many cities in India, with a similar climate, though it was almost always humid. The school was a lot like mine back in Hyderabad. The people were what stood out. They were more driven, more passionate and more focused on the goals they had set themselves.

Almost everyone was involved in school activities and clubs. Many ran side projects too, from YouTube channels to science projects they wanted to take to a national level, all while striving for academic excellence. Working without watching the clock was not unusual, and that really struck me.

When I asked my friends what drove them so hard, they said, "everyone else does it and it seems like they have fun so I wanted to try things of my own".

That has stayed with me. You don't need a grand story about overcoming hardship, or about having everything and still wanting more. They simply enjoyed the people around them, and they helped each other grow.

Constructive criticism was commonplace there, which I had to adapt to, along with the teaching style, the climate and, ever so slightly, the culture. It was quite similar to ours back home: very community focused, with importance placed on future careers and lasting relationships.

I went there empty handed, ready to learn, and boy did I. I came home having learned to take care of myself in a different country, away from my parents. I learned to deal with different kinds of friendship, and I understood more about how I learn. Most importantly, I learned to be more curious and creative. The hack I found was to surround myself with like-minded people, and I naturally grew more competent.

My favourite example is a chemistry presentation about an element of our choice and its impact on the world around us. I first chose something simple, which I can't even remember now. Then I wanted to challenge myself, so I picked curium, because I was very interested in space exploration and had been reading about how we were managing to get the power to do it.

I remember spending a lot of time with my teacher, testing my understanding of curium, what it means for our ability to explore space, and what chain effects it could have on the environment and the economy.

Looking at it now, the slides seem clumsy and messy. But I strongly recall the drive I had to fully explore this element, understand its use and share with the class why it was so cool.
