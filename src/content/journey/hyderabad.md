In 2016 we moved to Hyderabad, for family reasons. We had visited India almost every year since moving to Sweden, so India was not exactly new to me. But trying to coexist with the people definitely was. Hyderabad was the catalyst for who I am today.

## Arriving

I arrived as a non-resident Indian who had spent most of his life in Sweden, and I felt like an imposter. Thankfully, my parents had made sure I spoke Telugu, but I didn't speak any Hindi. My school was international and most people spoke English, but a lot of the casual banter happened in Hindi, and I felt left out.

For the first few years I had a small, tight-knit group of friends I could count on one hand, a few at the apartments and a few at school, and I played a lot of basketball and badminton. From August to December 2019 I was away in Kenya, which has its own chapter. Then Covid hit and school moved online. Almost a year in, I realised I was yearning to know more people. I had spent a lot of time with my classmates, but we were only ever friends by happenstance. I wanted to be friends with them because I made the effort to.

## Saying yes on purpose

So when the restrictions lifted in 2021, I joined my school's residential programme, and my two IB years were the best time of my life. Academics were the priority there, and I came out with a strong academic base. I picked up most of my Hindi there too, from the Hindi movies we watched and the people I spent my days with. At school we regularly celebrated Hindu and Muslim holidays together on a large scale, with cultural food, performances and dances.

I put myself out there: I tried new things and spoke to anyone and everyone, to find out what I liked doing and what I didn't. With every activity, I thought hard about why I wanted it. Was it to spend time with people, to try something new, to learn something, or simply to keep busy? I joined organic horticulture, waking up early to help on a small farm on campus, and the fruit and vegetables we grew went to the faculty living on campus. I had never thought about gardening before, but I really enjoyed the physical effort of nurturing them, and seeing what we grew made me really happy.

I always felt underprepared as a Model UN delegate, so I chaired our school's intramural MUN to see the other side, thinking it would be less work. It wasn't. I probably researched more as a chair than I ever had as a delegate: the issue, where every country stood, and a crisis for the committee with its ideal response. From the other side of the board I learned that preparation and facts impress, but thinking on your feet and confidence speak volumes.

## Asking for help

Towards the end of my first year in the dorms came the student council election. I went to a group of nine or ten boys in my class. We were friends, but I had never felt part of their group. They had wanted to stand themselves but couldn't because of the academic requirements, so asking for their help was a hard sell. I told them I wanted to do good work at school, so that we could all enjoy more, explore more and experience more, and that if they thought I was capable of it, I would love their support.

They asked for time to talk it over, so I waited outside their room. When they called me back in, they had two things to say. Was I going to work for the betterment of our experience at school? And: "Well man, you are one of us, even if you only joined this year. You're like our brother, so of course we'll help you."

That day I realised I had misunderstood completely. They weren't excluding me; I had just never tried to include myself. From then on I spent more time with them, bothered them more, and came to feel part of the fold, with people I am proud to call my friends, brothers even. We still speak every other week.

I made sure to spend just as much time with everyone else too, students in the grades above and below me. I think my habit of speaking to everyone and anyone, and not at a superficial level, is what got me elected student council president.

## What I did with it

As president I ran drug awareness sessions, helped organise Teachers' Day, Cultural Day and sports events, and set up student representatives across middle and high school so more ideas reached the council.

As an external affairs intern, I raised money for scholarships for underprivileged students. I was part of the first group that worked out how: the events, the online push and the pitch. I called and met donors, and led some of the in-person stalls. On one open day we set up on the boulevard at the school entrance and spoke to almost 60 sets of parents. Together we raised more than $40,000.

I was part of a lot of things, but I remained a friend. When friends were going through something, I took the time to sit with them and sort it out, and if I wasn't the right person to help, I found someone who was. At one point I went through a time of not knowing what kind of friend I was, and talked to my school counsellor. She asked me one question: "Why do you feel the need to label your role in your friendships?" I realised I had never worked like that. If I consider you a friend, you are someone whose opinion I value, who I would help in trouble, and who I like to hang out with. Nothing in that needs a label.

## Leaving

It all came together when I was writing my high school graduation speech. Looking back at the fun times, the trials and the learnings, I realised I wasn't just leaving with good grades and some friends. I was leaving with years of understanding what a community is, what it means to do service, and what it means to celebrate our culture together.

If I could tell my 2016 self one thing, it wouldn't be to change anything. It would be: you're in for one hell of a ride, so just have fun. I left in 2023 able to enter a new environment, live by myself, learn and perform.
